\section{Discussion}

To answer the research question directly: on this cohort the raw multichannel
window is the most informative of the four representations for binary
engagement inference in unseen office workers, with handcrafted features close
behind and the two frozen embeddings improving on neither. The margin over the
majority baseline is, however, small for every candidate, so the more robust
reading of Table~\ref{tab:main} is that three seconds of gaze does not make
momentary self-reported engagement well predictable across people, whichever
representation is used. The representation effect, modest as it is, is still
larger and more consistent than the classifier effect, which reorders nothing
except the sign of the two embedding results.

The failure of the frozen encoders to improve on simpler inputs is best read
as a mismatch between pretraining and task. GazeMAE was pretrained on
controlled stimulus-viewing corpora and MOMENT on generic time-series
benchmarks; neither has seen day-long, spontaneous office gaze with its
dropouts, idle stretches and heterogeneous displays. Using them out of the box
is therefore not a verdict on representation learning for eye tracking, but it
is the first thing a practitioner tries, and in this setting it does not pay
off. Domain adaptation or fine-tuning on in-domain gaze, rather than frozen
inference, is the natural next step---particularly for GazeMAE, whose
classifier-dependent behaviour (Section~\ref{sec:results}) leaves its usefulness here
genuinely open.

For practice, raw signals are the strict empirical winner and also the
cheapest option: no feature engineering, no pretrained checkpoint, no GPU
inference pass, and 600 dimensions against MOMENT's 1{,}024. We would not
generalise this into a recommendation to abandon handcrafted features. They
finished a close second, they remain interpretable, and our earlier
feature-based analyses found them effective on related eye-tracking targets
\cite{bozak2024emotion,bozak2026workload}, so they are still worth trying on a
similar problem. What we would not recommend is reaching for a frozen
general-purpose encoder as a default.

Several limitations bound these conclusions. The study uses one unpublished
and still-growing dataset with 17 participants; the target is a noisy
self-report assigned retrospectively to a 15-minute interval and binarised by
within-participant centring; and the results depend on one windowing scheme,
one seed, one set of quality-control rules and two specific public
checkpoints. The density map and the projections are descriptive only. Future
work should validate the comparison on further datasets, adapt or fine-tune
the encoders on in-domain gaze, explore calibrated personalisation for new
users, and test longer windows and aggregated targets that match the
granularity at which engagement is actually reported.

\section{Conclusion}

We compared four eye-tracking representations---raw multichannel windows,
handcrafted ocular features, frozen GazeMAE embeddings and frozen MOMENT
embeddings---under one identical, leakage-safe leave-one-subject-out protocol
for momentary engagement inference in office workers. The simplest and
cheapest representation was also the strongest, reaching 0.538 accuracy and
0.551 ROC-AUC on unseen participants, and neither pretrained encoder repaid
its additional cost out of the box. For practitioners working with limited
labelled data, the evidence here favours starting from raw windows or
handcrafted features and treating frozen foundation-model embeddings as
something to be adapted before they are trusted.
