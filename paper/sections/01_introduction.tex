\section{Introduction}

Interactive systems that adapt to the person using them need evidence about that person's momentary state.
In knowledge work, engagement---how absorbed someone is in the task at hand---is an attractive target: it fluctuates within a working day, it is only weakly visible in application logs, and it is the state most directly tied to productivity and well-being interventions.
Eye tracking is a plausible sensor for it.
Gaze position, fixation and saccade dynamics, blinks and pupil size are available continuously from a screen-mounted tracker, require no action from the user, and have documented links to attention and processing load.

Turning a stream of gaze samples into model input, however, forces a design decision that is rarely examined on its own.
A short window of eye-tracking data can be presented to a classifier in at least four ways.
The raw multichannel signal retains everything the tracker measured, at the price of high dimensionality and no structure.
Handcrafted ocular features---fixation, saccade, blink and pupil statistics---encode decades of domain knowledge, stay interpretable, and remain the default choice in applied work \cite{bozak2024emotion,bozak2026workload}.
Learned representations promise to remove the manual step: GazeMAE \cite{bautista2020gazemae} provides gaze-specific embeddings pretrained on eye-movement corpora, and general-purpose time-series foundation models such as MOMENT \cite{goswami2024moment} provide a frozen encoder intended to transfer to unseen domains.
The four options differ substantially in preprocessing effort, storage and compute, and practitioners have little evidence on which to base the choice.

That evidence gap exists because the families are seldom compared like for like.
Published results differ in windows, labels, classifiers and, most consequentially, in validation protocol, so an apparent advantage of one representation can just as easily come from an optimistic split.
What an applied designer needs is the comparison under the condition that matters for deployment: a model trained on some people and applied to a person it has never seen.

We therefore ask which eye-tracking representation supports the most reliable inference of self-reported momentary engagement in unseen office workers.
We answer with a controlled comparison of raw signals, handcrafted features, GazeMAE embeddings and MOMENT embeddings on identical three-second windows, an identical binary engagement target and identical leave-one-subject-out folds.
Both pretrained encoders are kept frozen and only a prediction head is trained, which is the out-of-the-box setting a practitioner would try first.
Our contributions are (i) a leakage-safe, subject-disjoint comparison of the four representation families on spontaneous office work, and (ii) cost-aware guidance for representation selection under limited labelled data.
