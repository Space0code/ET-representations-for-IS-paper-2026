\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Data and Eye-Tracking Representations}

The data came from a study conducting multimodal data collection, in which knowledge workers were recorded during their daily routine at their own workplace.
The data being collected includes RGB video, eye tracking, microphone audio and application usage.
This paper uses the eye-tracking modality only.
Data collection is ongoing and the dataset is not yet public.
This preliminary study used the cohort available at the time of writing, which includes 17 participants and 657 full-workday recordings, with 19 to 79 recordings per participant (median 32).
The tracker sampled at 60\,Hz, and we retained five channels: left and right pupil size, gaze $x$ and $y$, and the average eye--screen distance.

Labels came from an experience-sampling protocol, in which participants rated their momentary state in situ rather than recalling it afterwards.
Participants were prompted several times per working day with a short questionnaire covering engagement, affect, fatigue, stress, physical comfort and workplace interactions.
Our target was the immersion item, \enquote{Right now I am immersed in my work}, one of three momentary engagement items answered at every prompt and rated on a seven-point scale from 0 to 6.
We refer to it as the \emph{engagement rating}.
Each prompt labelled the 15 minutes of recording that preceded it, a labelling horizon we chose ourselves, and only those intervals entered this study.

Recordings were segmented into fixed three-second windows with a 2.25-second step (25\,\% overlap).
The four representation exports were joined on a per-window identifier, so all models saw exactly the same windows.
At 60\,Hz, each window was expected to contain 180 frames, although sampling gaps sometimes reduced this number.
A window was kept only if at least 30\,\% of its recorded frames were valid and at least 32 valid frames were available.
These permissive thresholds accommodated frequent tracking loss in unconstrained office work.
The 32-frame minimum corresponds to about 0.53 seconds at 60\,Hz, which can include two fixations of typical duration \cite{rayner1998reading}.
Gaps inside retained windows were linearly interpolated.
Of roughly 7.3 million recorded windows, only those inside a labelled interval are usable.
Handcrafted features were available for 177{,}244 of them, 157{,}685 of those also had both encoder embeddings, and 140{,}531 carried a numeric engagement rating.
The final modelling cohort accounts for about 2\,\% of all recorded windows and contains between 2{,}136 and 25{,}447 windows per participant.

Display resolutions ranged from 1920$\times$1080 to 3840$\times$2160 pixels and differed between participants, and one participant used two resolutions across recordings.
For the raw representation, gaze coordinates were normalised to $[0,1]$ using each source recording's declared resolution, and out-of-screen coordinates were retained.
Overall, 94.7\,\% of samples fell on the display, and only those entered the descriptive density map in Figure~\ref{fig:descriptive}(a), which shows the expected central concentration together with broad coverage of the screen.

Four representations were computed from these windows.
Coordinates are display-normalised for the raw export and in visual-angle units for GazeMAE; the handcrafted features retain pixel coordinates.
(1) \textbf{Raw signals} use the five channels, each linearly resampled to 120 samples so that the whole window is covered, flattened into a 600-dimensional vector.
(2) \textbf{Handcrafted features} describe a window through the ocular events and signals it contains, following the feature set of our earlier eye-tracking work \cite{bozak2026workload}.
Blinks, fixations and saccades are taken from the tracker's event classification, and each event type contributes counts, rates and duration statistics, together with the location and dispersion of fixations and the amplitude and velocity of saccades.
The remaining groups summarise pupil size and its rate of change, the eye--screen distance and its dynamics, and the fraction of missing samples in each channel.
This gives 181 numeric features per window, which were filtered inside each fold as described in Section~\ref{subsec:design}.
Window identifiers, timestamps and questionnaire columns were not used as model input.
(3) \textbf{GazeMAE embeddings} were computed with the public micro--macro autoencoder checkpoints applied to gaze position and velocity resampled to 500\,Hz, with the 128-dimensional position and velocity codes concatenated into 256 dimensions \cite{bautista2020gazemae}.
Following the encoder's own preprocessing, coordinates were rescaled to 35 pixels per degree of visual angle using each recording's display resolution and the measured eye--screen distance, assuming the standard physical size for each resolution.
(4) \textbf{MOMENT embeddings} were calculated with the \textsc{MOMENT-1-large} encoder applied to the five-channel windows before display-coordinate normalisation, with an input length of 512 and mean pooling, giving 1{,}024 dimensions \cite{goswami2024moment}.
Its encoder standardises each channel per window, so the input coordinate scale is immaterial.
Both pretrained encoders were used out of the box and kept frozen, with no weight updates.

\subsection{Experimental Design}
\label{subsec:design}

Engagement ratings were strongly person-specific, with participant means spanning 2.2 to 4.9 on the 0--6 scale (Figure~\ref{fig:descriptive}(b)).
An absolute threshold would therefore mostly encode participant identity.
Inside every fold, the ratings of each training participant were mean-centred separately.
The held-out participant's ratings were centred with the unweighted mean of the training participants' means, which avoids using held-out labels to set the threshold.
A window is positive when its centred rating exceeds zero.
Across the left-out folds, 81{,}341 of the 140{,}531 windows (57.9\,\%) were positive, and the participant-specific rates ranged from 0\,\% to 95\,\%.

The primary protocol was subject-disjoint cross-validation, with one of the 17 participants held out in each fold.
Everything data-dependent was fitted inside the fold.
Handcrafted-feature filtering, median imputation and standardisation were all fitted on the training participants only and then applied with unchanged parameters to the held-out participant.
The filtering drops columns with more than 60\,\% missing values, zero variance, or absolute Pearson correlation above 0.8 with a retained column, leaving 58--60 of the 181 feature candidates.

Three predictors were run on every representation: a majority baseline, a random forest (300 trees, balanced class weights) and an MLP (1028--512--256--128--64 units, GELU activations, layer normalisation, Adam with learning rate $2\times10^{-4}$ and weight decay $10^{-3}$, batch size 1024, at most 200 epochs with early stopping after 15 epochs without improvement).
For the MLP, a stratified 20\,\% split of the outer training windows was used for early stopping.
A single seed was used throughout.

We report balanced accuracy as the primary metric: because the positive rate varies so strongly between participants, plain accuracy partly measures a participant's own base rate, while balanced accuracy has a fixed chance level of 0.500 that makes folds comparable.
Accuracy, together with its improvement over the majority baseline paired within folds, and macro-F1 and ROC-AUC are reported as secondary measures.
All metrics were aggregated as unweighted means over participants.
One participant self-reported every window below the training centre, so that test fold contains a single class.
Balanced accuracy, macro-F1 and ROC-AUC are therefore defined for 16 folds and accuracy for all 17.
As a separate within-participant check, we also ran a one-step persistence predictor within recordings, carrying the last observed label forward in 90-window test blocks after at least 300 calibration windows.
